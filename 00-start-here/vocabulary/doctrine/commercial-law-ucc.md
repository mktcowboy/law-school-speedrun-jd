# Commercial Law And The UCC Vocabulary

Back to the [doctrine vocabulary](./README.md) · [vocabulary overview](../README.md).

Terms that recur in sales, negotiable instruments, and secured-transactions problems under the Uniform Commercial Code.

### `Article 2`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

Article 2 is the UCC article governing transactions in goods. It modifies common-law contract rules for merchants, warranties, acceptance, rejection, breach, and remedies.

*In use:* A sale of widgets is analyzed under Article 2, while a contract for legal services is not.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/uniform_commercial_code) · related: goods, merchant, perfect tender rule

### `attachment`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

Attachment is the moment a security interest becomes enforceable against the debtor. It generally requires value, the debtor's rights in the collateral, and an authenticated security agreement or possession/control of the collateral.

*In use:* The lender had no enforceable security interest until attachment occurred.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/security_interest) · related: security interest, collateral, perfection

### `battle of the forms`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

The battle of the forms is the UCC problem that arises when merchants exchange forms with different or additional terms. Article 2 decides whether a contract formed and which terms became part of it.

*In use:* The buyer's purchase order and seller's invoice disagreed on arbitration, creating a battle-of-the-forms issue.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/uniform_commercial_code) · related: merchant, additional terms, firm offer

### `buyer in ordinary course of business`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

A buyer in ordinary course of business buys goods in good faith from a seller who regularly deals in goods of that kind. That buyer often takes free of a security interest created by the seller.

*In use:* A customer who buys a laptop from an electronics store usually qualifies as a buyer in ordinary course.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/uniform_commercial_code) · related: good faith, security interest, entrustment

### `collateral`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

Collateral is property subject to a security interest that secures payment or performance of an obligation. If the debtor defaults, the secured party may have rights against the collateral.

*In use:* The inventory served as collateral for the merchant's revolving line of credit.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/secured_transaction) · related: debtor, secured party, security interest

### `cover`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

Cover is a buyer's purchase of substitute goods after the seller breaches. If done reasonably, the buyer may recover the difference between the cover price and the contract price plus incidental or consequential damages.

*In use:* After the seller failed to deliver, the buyer covered by buying replacement grain on the open market.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/uniform_commercial_code) · related: breach, damages, perfect tender rule

### `entrustment`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

Entrustment occurs when an owner gives goods to a merchant who deals in goods of that kind. Under the UCC, the merchant may transfer the owner's rights to a buyer in ordinary course.

*In use:* Leaving a watch with a jeweler for repair can create an entrustment problem if the jeweler sells it.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/uniform_commercial_code) · related: buyer in ordinary course, good faith, merchant

### `express warranty`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

An express warranty is a seller's affirmation, promise, description, sample, or model that becomes part of the basis of the bargain. It gives the buyer a contractual assurance about the goods.

*In use:* The seller's statement that the machine could process 500 units per hour may create an express warranty.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/express_warranty) · related: warranty, implied warranty of merchantability, basis of the bargain

### `financing statement`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

A financing statement is the public filing used to give notice that a secured party may have an interest in the debtor's collateral. Filing one is the usual way to perfect many Article 9 security interests.

*In use:* The bank filed a financing statement naming the debtor and describing the equipment collateral.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/secured_transaction) · related: perfection, priority, security interest

### `firm offer`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

A firm offer is a merchant's signed promise to keep an offer to buy or sell goods open. Under Article 2, it can be irrevocable for a limited time even without consideration.

*In use:* The supplier's signed quote promised to hold the price open for 60 days, making it a firm offer.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/firm_offer) · related: merchant, offer, Article 2

### `good faith`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

Good faith means honesty in fact and, for merchants, observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing. The UCC uses it as a baseline obligation across commercial transactions.

*In use:* A secured party must dispose of collateral in good faith and in a commercially reasonable manner.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/good_faith) · related: merchant, buyer in ordinary course, commercial reasonableness

### `holder in due course`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

A holder in due course takes a negotiable instrument for value, in good faith, and without notice of certain problems. That status cuts off many defenses that could be raised against the original payee.

*In use:* A bank that takes a check without notice of fraud may argue it is a holder in due course.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/holder_in_due_course) · related: negotiable instrument, value, good faith

### `implied warranty of fitness`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

The implied warranty of fitness arises when a seller knows the buyer's particular purpose and knows the buyer is relying on the seller's skill or judgment. It promises that the goods are fit for that special purpose.

*In use:* A seller who recommends paint for marine use may create an implied warranty of fitness for that purpose.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/implied_warranty_of_fitness) · related: warranty, reliance, implied warranty of merchantability

### `implied warranty of merchantability`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

The implied warranty of merchantability promises that goods sold by a merchant are fit for ordinary purposes and pass without objection in the trade. It attaches automatically unless effectively disclaimed.

*In use:* A toaster that catches fire during normal use likely breaches the implied warranty of merchantability.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/implied_warranty_of_merchantability) · related: merchant, warranty, disclaimer

### `merchant`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

A merchant is someone who deals in goods of the kind or otherwise holds themselves out as having special commercial knowledge or skill. Merchant status triggers several UCC rules that do not apply to casual sellers.

*In use:* A hardware store is a merchant for the sale of drills, but a homeowner selling one used drill is usually not.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/uniform_commercial_code) · related: Article 2, good faith, firm offer

### `negotiable instrument`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

A negotiable instrument is a signed writing that contains an unconditional promise or order to pay a fixed amount of money. Checks and promissory notes are the standard examples.

*In use:* If the note is negotiable, the holder-in-due-course rules may matter.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/negotiable_instrument) · related: holder in due course, note, check

### `perfect tender rule`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

The perfect tender rule lets a buyer reject goods if the goods or tender of delivery fail in any respect to conform to the contract. The rule is softened by doctrines like cure, acceptance, and installment-contract rules.

*In use:* A shipment of nonconforming goods triggers the buyer's perfect-tender options.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/perfect_tender_rule) · related: rejection, cure, revocation of acceptance

### `perfection`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

Perfection is the step that protects a secured party's interest against third parties. Depending on the collateral, perfection may occur by filing, possession, control, or sometimes automatically.

*In use:* Attachment made the security interest enforceable against the debtor, but perfection determined priority against other creditors.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/security_interest) · related: financing statement, priority, secured transaction

### `priority`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

Priority determines which claimant has the superior right to collateral or proceeds when multiple parties assert interests. Article 9 often rewards the first secured party to file or perfect, subject to special rules.

*In use:* The dispute between two lenders was a priority fight over the same inventory collateral.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/secured_transaction) · related: perfection, purchase-money security interest, lien creditor

### `purchase-money security interest`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

A purchase-money security interest, or PMSI, secures credit used to acquire the collateral itself. Article 9 gives PMSIs special priority if the secured party follows the statute's timing and notice rules.

*In use:* A seller who finances the buyer's purchase of equipment may retain a PMSI in that equipment.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/secured_transaction) · related: priority, security interest, collateral

### `rejection`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

Rejection is the buyer's refusal to accept nonconforming goods. The buyer must reject within a reasonable time and seasonably notify the seller.

*In use:* The buyer rejected the shipment after discovering that the goods did not match the contract specifications.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/perfect_tender_rule) · related: acceptance, cover, revocation of acceptance

### `revocation of acceptance`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

Revocation of acceptance lets a buyer undo an earlier acceptance when a substantial nonconformity later emerges or was difficult to discover. It is harder than initial rejection because the buyer has already accepted the goods.

*In use:* The buyer revoked acceptance after repeated repair attempts failed to fix the equipment.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/uniform_commercial_code) · related: rejection, substantial impairment, perfect tender rule

### `secured transaction`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

A secured transaction is a credit transaction in which the debtor grants a security interest in collateral to secure an obligation. Article 9 governs most secured transactions in personal property.

*In use:* A bank loan secured by the borrower's accounts receivable is a secured transaction.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/secured_transaction) · related: debtor, collateral, secured party

### `security interest`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

A security interest is a property interest in collateral that secures payment or performance of an obligation. It gives the secured party rights if the debtor defaults.

*In use:* The lender took a security interest in the debtor's equipment and inventory.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/security_interest) · related: attachment, perfection, collateral

### `statute of frauds`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

The statute of frauds requires certain agreements to be evidenced by a writing. Under Article 2, contracts for the sale of goods at or above the statutory amount usually need a sufficient writing unless an exception applies.

*In use:* The buyer invoked the statute of frauds because the alleged oral goods contract exceeded the UCC threshold.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/statute_of_frauds) · related: Article 2, writing, merchant's confirmation

### `Uniform Commercial Code`

**Commercial Law and the UCC**

The Uniform Commercial Code is a model code adopted by states to make commercial law more uniform. It covers sales of goods, leases, negotiable instruments, bank deposits, letters of credit, bulk sales, documents of title, investment securities, and secured transactions.

*In use:* Because each state enacts the UCC as state law, always check the jurisdiction's version.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/uniform_commercial_code) · related: Article 2, Article 9, commercial transaction
