# Administrative Law Vocabulary

Back to the [doctrine vocabulary](./README.md) · [vocabulary overview](../README.md).

Terms that recur in administrative law casebooks, agency materials, and judicial-review problems.

### `Administrative Procedure Act`

**Administrative Law**

The Administrative Procedure Act, or APA, is the federal statute that sets default procedures for agency rulemaking, adjudication, and judicial review. It is the backbone for most federal administrative law problems.

*In use:* A plaintiff challenging an agency rule usually starts by asking whether the APA gives a cause of action and a standard of review.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/administrative_procedure_act) · related: agency action, rulemaking, judicial review

### `administrative agency`

**Administrative Law**

An administrative agency is a government body created by statute to administer, enforce, or implement a regulatory program. Agencies often combine investigation, rulemaking, adjudication, and enforcement functions.

*In use:* The EPA, SEC, and NLRB are administrative agencies with authority delegated by Congress.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/administrative_law) · related: delegation, organic statute, rulemaking

### `adjudication`

**Administrative Law**

Adjudication is an agency process for deciding specific disputes or claims, usually by applying law to particular facts. It can look trial-like in formal proceedings or more streamlined in informal ones.

*In use:* A benefits hearing before an administrative law judge is a common form of agency adjudication.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/adjudication) · related: formal adjudication, due process, substantial evidence

### `arbitrary and capricious review`

**Administrative Law**

Arbitrary and capricious review asks whether an agency gave a reasoned explanation, considered the important issues, and stayed within the bounds of its authority. It is deferential, but not toothless.

*In use:* A court may set aside a rule as arbitrary and capricious if the agency ignored an obvious alternative or contradicted its own evidence.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/administrative_law) · related: judicial review, hard look review, reasoned decisionmaking

### `Chevron deference`

**Administrative Law**

Chevron deference was the doctrine under which courts deferred to reasonable agency interpretations of ambiguous statutes the agency administered. After *Loper Bright* in 2024, federal courts no longer apply Chevron as binding deference, but the term remains essential for reading older cases and understanding the field.

*In use:* An exam may ask how an agency would have fared under Chevron and how the analysis changes after *Loper Bright*.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/chevron_deference) · related: Skidmore deference, statutory interpretation, Loper Bright

### `delegation`

**Administrative Law**

Delegation is Congress's transfer of authority to an agency to fill in details, make rules, enforce statutes, or decide cases. Most administrative law begins from the scope and limits of that delegated power.

*In use:* A court asks whether Congress delegated enough authority for the agency to regulate the disputed conduct.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/administrative_law) · related: nondelegation doctrine, organic statute, major questions doctrine

### `exhaustion of administrative remedies`

**Administrative Law**

Exhaustion requires a party to use the agency's available procedures before going to court. The doctrine gives the agency a chance to correct errors and build a record before judicial review.

*In use:* A regulated party may have to finish the administrative appeal process before filing an APA suit.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/administrative_law) · related: final agency action, ripeness, judicial review

### `final agency action`

**Administrative Law**

Final agency action is agency conduct that marks the end of the agency's decisionmaking process and has legal consequences for the parties. Without finality, a federal APA challenge is often premature.

*In use:* A warning letter may not be final agency action if it does not determine rights or obligations.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/administrative_law) · related: ripeness, exhaustion, judicial review

### `formal adjudication`

**Administrative Law**

Formal adjudication is an APA proceeding required by statute to be decided on the record after an agency hearing. It usually includes trial-like procedures before an administrative law judge.

*In use:* When a statute triggers formal adjudication, the parties may present evidence and cross-examine witnesses.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/adjudication) · related: administrative law judge, substantial evidence, due process

### `guidance document`

**Administrative Law**

A guidance document states an agency's interpretation, policy, or recommended approach without itself carrying the force of law. The hard question is whether the agency is really using guidance as a binding rule.

*In use:* A regulated company may challenge guidance if the agency treats it as mandatory in practice.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/administrative_law) · related: interpretive rule, policy statement, notice-and-comment rulemaking

### `hard look review`

**Administrative Law**

Hard look review is a more searching form of arbitrary-and-capricious review, especially for rulemaking records. The court checks whether the agency confronted the evidence, explained its choices, and responded to serious objections.

*In use:* Environmental and technical rulemakings often invite hard look review of the agency's reasoning.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/administrative_law) · related: arbitrary and capricious review, rulemaking record, reasoned decisionmaking

### `informal rulemaking`

**Administrative Law**

Informal rulemaking is the APA's notice-and-comment process for adopting rules without a trial-like hearing. It is the everyday mechanism for most federal regulations.

*In use:* The agency published a proposed rule, received comments, and then issued a final rule through informal rulemaking.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/rulemaking) · related: notice-and-comment rulemaking, final rule, Federal Register

### `judicial review`

**Administrative Law**

Judicial review is a court's examination of agency action for legality, procedure, reasoned explanation, and support in the record. Administrative law problems often turn on whether review is available and how intense it should be.

*In use:* The APA creates a default path for judicial review unless a statute precludes review or commits the issue to agency discretion.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/administrative_law) · related: arbitrary and capricious review, substantial evidence, final agency action

### `major questions doctrine`

**Administrative Law**

The major questions doctrine requires clear congressional authorization when an agency claims broad power over issues of great economic or political significance. It limits agencies from finding sweeping authority in vague or ancillary statutory language.

*In use:* A court may reject a novel agency program if it treats the question as too major for implied delegation.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/administrative_law) · related: delegation, statutory interpretation, nondelegation doctrine

### `notice-and-comment rulemaking`

**Administrative Law**

Notice-and-comment rulemaking is the APA process in which an agency proposes a rule, invites public comments, considers the record, and publishes a final rule with reasons. It is designed to make regulation more transparent and participatory.

*In use:* A final rule can fail if it is not a logical outgrowth of the proposal or if the agency ignores important comments.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/rulemaking) · related: informal rulemaking, Federal Register, arbitrary and capricious review

### `nondelegation doctrine`

**Administrative Law**

The nondelegation doctrine limits Congress's ability to give legislative power away without an intelligible principle to guide the agency. Modern doctrine is permissive, but the concept frames many separation-of-powers attacks on agencies.

*In use:* A challenger may argue that a statute gives the agency standardless power to decide major policy questions.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/nondelegation_doctrine) · related: delegation, separation of powers, major questions doctrine

### `organic statute`

**Administrative Law**

An organic statute is the statute that creates an agency or gives it the authority at issue. It defines the agency's mission, tools, limits, and sometimes its procedures.

*In use:* Before evaluating an agency rule, read the organic statute to see what Congress actually authorized.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/administrative_law) · related: delegation, administrative agency, statutory authority

### `ripeness`

**Administrative Law**

Ripeness asks whether a dispute is ready for judicial decision or still too abstract, tentative, or contingent. In administrative law, courts often balance the fitness of the issues for review against hardship to the parties.

*In use:* A pre-enforcement challenge may be ripe if the rule is final and compliance costs are immediate.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/justiciability) · related: final agency action, standing, exhaustion

### `rulemaking`

**Administrative Law**

Rulemaking is the agency process for creating rules of general future effect. It is usually legislative in character because the agency sets standards that apply across a class of people or conduct.

*In use:* Agencies use rulemaking to translate broad statutory commands into operational regulations.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/rulemaking) · related: adjudication, notice-and-comment rulemaking, regulation

### `separation of functions`

**Administrative Law**

Separation of functions is the idea that agency prosecutors, investigators, and adjudicators should be kept sufficiently apart in formal proceedings. It protects the fairness and appearance of impartiality in agency adjudication.

*In use:* A party may object if the same agency official both prosecutes the case and advises the decisionmaker.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/administrative_law) · related: due process, formal adjudication, administrative law judge

### `Skidmore deference`

**Administrative Law**

Skidmore deference gives agency interpretations weight according to their persuasiveness, consistency, thoroughness, and fit with the agency's expertise. It is not binding deference; the court remains responsible for deciding the law.

*In use:* After *Loper Bright*, agency interpretations may still matter under Skidmore if they are carefully reasoned.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/administrative_law) · related: Chevron deference, statutory interpretation, agency expertise

### `standing`

**Administrative Law**

Standing is the requirement that the plaintiff have a concrete injury traceable to the defendant and likely redressable by the court. In agency cases, standing often turns on whether regulation, deregulation, or procedural failure caused a real injury.

*In use:* A public-interest group must show that at least one member is actually harmed by the challenged agency action.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/standing) · related: ripeness, judicial review, injury in fact

### `substantial evidence`

**Administrative Law**

Substantial evidence is the level of record support needed for certain agency factual findings, especially after formal proceedings. The question is whether a reasonable mind could accept the evidence as adequate.

*In use:* A court reviewing a formal adjudication may uphold the agency's findings if substantial evidence supports them.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/administrative_law) · related: formal adjudication, record review, judicial review

### `ultra vires`

**Administrative Law** · lit. "beyond the powers"

Ultra vires agency action exceeds the authority granted by statute or the Constitution. The label matters because agencies have only the powers law gives them.

*In use:* A court may vacate an agency order as ultra vires if the organic statute never authorized it.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/ultra_vires) · related: delegation, organic statute, statutory authority
