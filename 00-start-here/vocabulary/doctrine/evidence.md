# Evidence Vocabulary

Back to the [doctrine vocabulary index](./README.md) · [vocabulary overview](../README.md).

High-frequency terms for Evidence: admissibility, proof, witnesses, privileges, and the hearsay maze.

### `admissible evidence`

**Evidence**

Evidence the court allows the jury or judge to consider. Even relevant evidence can be excluded if another rule bars it.

*In use:* The text messages were relevant, but the court still had to decide whether they were admissible evidence.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/admissible_evidence) · related: relevant evidence, authentication

### `authentication`

**Evidence**

The showing that an item is what its proponent claims it is. In practice, authentication often means laying enough foundation for a reasonable juror to believe the exhibit is genuine.

*In use:* The witness authenticated the contract by identifying her signature.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/authentication) · related: foundation, exhibit

### `best evidence rule`

**Evidence**

The rule that, when a party seeks to prove the contents of a writing, recording, or photograph, the original or a reliable duplicate is usually required. It does not apply when the witness is testifying about an event rather than the contents of a document.

*In use:* The best evidence rule mattered because the plaintiff was trying to prove the exact words in the letter.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/best_evidence_rule) · related: authentication, admissible evidence

### `burden of proof`

**Evidence**

The obligation to prove a fact or claim to the required level of certainty. It includes both the burden of production and the burden of persuasion.

*In use:* The plaintiff carried the burden of proof on causation.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/burden_of_proof) · related: preponderance, clear and convincing evidence

### `character evidence`

**Evidence**

Evidence offered to show a person's trait or disposition and then argue they acted consistently with it. The rules often bar propensity use, but allow character evidence for limited purposes in specific situations.

*In use:* The prosecution could not use character evidence simply to show the defendant was the kind of person who would steal.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/character_evidence) · related: propensity, impeachment

### `cross-examination`

**Evidence**

Questioning a witness after the other side has called that witness. Cross-examination tests perception, memory, credibility, and the limits of the direct testimony.

*In use:* Cross-examination exposed that the witness saw the accident only after hearing the crash.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/cross-examination) · related: direct examination, leading question

### `direct examination`

**Evidence**

The first questioning of a witness by the party who called the witness. Direct examination usually uses open-ended, non-leading questions to build the witness's account.

*In use:* On direct examination, the officer described where the evidence was found.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/direct_examination) · related: cross-examination, testimony

### `dying declaration`

**Evidence**

A hearsay exception for a statement made while the declarant believed death was imminent, concerning the cause or circumstances of that death. It is narrow and turns heavily on the declarant's settled expectation of death.

*In use:* The victim's final identification was offered as a dying declaration.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/dying_declaration) · related: hearsay, declarant

### `excited utterance`

**Evidence**

A hearsay exception for a statement about a startling event made while the speaker is still under the stress of that event. The theory is that stress leaves little time to fabricate.

*In use:* The 911 call came in seconds after the collision, so the statement fit the excited utterance exception.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/excited_utterance) · related: hearsay, present sense impression

### `expert witness`

**Evidence**

A witness qualified by knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education to give opinion testimony that will help the factfinder. Expert testimony must be reliable and tied to the facts of the case.

*In use:* The engineer testified as an expert witness on the bridge design.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/expert_witness) · related: opinion testimony, lay witness

### `foundation`

**Evidence**

The preliminary facts needed before evidence can be admitted. A lawyer "lays foundation" by showing relevance, authenticity, witness knowledge, or other rule-based prerequisites.

*In use:* Before showing the photo to the jury, counsel laid foundation through the officer who took it.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/authentication) · related: authentication, admissible evidence

### `hearsay`

**Evidence**

An out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of what it asserts. Hearsay is generally inadmissible unless an exclusion or exception applies.

*In use:* The email was hearsay when offered to prove the shipment arrived late.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/hearsay) · related: declarant, excited utterance

### `judicial notice`

**Evidence**

A court's acceptance of a fact as true without formal proof because the fact is not reasonably disputed. Common examples are calendar dates, geographic facts, or matters shown by reliable public sources.

*In use:* The court took judicial notice that July 4 fell on a Tuesday that year.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/judicial_notice) · related: adjudicative fact, proof

### `lay witness`

**Evidence**

A non-expert witness who testifies from personal knowledge. A lay witness may give limited opinions when they are rationally based on perception and helpful to understanding the testimony.

*In use:* The neighbor testified as a lay witness that the driver appeared intoxicated.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/witness) · related: expert witness, personal knowledge

### `leading question`

**Evidence**

A question that suggests its own answer. Leading questions are usually limited on direct examination but are common and useful on cross-examination.

*In use:* "The light was red, wasn't it?" was a leading question.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/leading_question) · related: direct examination, cross-examination

### `material evidence`

**Evidence**

Evidence that matters to a fact of consequence in the case. Materiality asks whether the fact helps prove or disprove something the law makes important.

*In use:* The timing of the phone call was material evidence on intent.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/relevant_evidence) · related: relevant evidence, probative value

### `offer of proof`

**Evidence**

A lawyer's record of what excluded evidence would have shown. It preserves the issue for appeal by giving the trial court and reviewing court the substance of the excluded proof.

*In use:* After the objection was sustained, counsel made an offer of proof outside the jury's hearing.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/evidence) · related: objection, appellate review

### `privilege`

**Evidence**

A rule that lets a person refuse to disclose protected communications or information. Privileges protect relationships or interests the law values, such as attorney-client confidentiality.

*In use:* The email was relevant, but attorney-client privilege kept it out.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/privilege) · related: attorney-client privilege, work product

### `relevant evidence`

**Evidence**

Evidence that tends to make a consequential fact more or less probable. Relevance is a low threshold, but relevant evidence may still be excluded for other reasons.

*In use:* The receipt was relevant evidence because it placed the defendant near the store.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/relevant_evidence) · related: material evidence, unfair prejudice

### `residual exception`

**Evidence**

A catchall hearsay exception for trustworthy statements not covered by a specific hearsay exception. It is used sparingly and usually requires notice to the other side.

*In use:* The report did not fit a named exception, so counsel tried the residual exception.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/hearsay) · related: hearsay, statement against interest

### `statement against interest`

**Evidence**

A hearsay exception for a statement so damaging to the speaker's own interest that a reasonable person would not have made it unless it were true. It usually requires the declarant to be unavailable.

*In use:* The accomplice's remark that he alone stole the car was offered as a statement against interest.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/hearsay) · related: hearsay, declarant

### `subsequent remedial measures`

**Evidence**

Repairs or safety changes made after an injury or accident. They are usually inadmissible to prove negligence or fault, though they may be admissible for another purpose such as ownership or feasibility.

*In use:* The new warning sign was excluded as a subsequent remedial measure.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/subsequent_remedial_measures) · related: negligence, admissible evidence

### `testimony`

**Evidence**

Evidence given by a witness under oath, either in court or in another formal setting such as a deposition. Testimony is only as strong as the witness's perception, memory, and credibility.

*In use:* The conviction rested mostly on eyewitness testimony.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/testimony) · related: witness, cross-examination

### `unfair prejudice`

**Evidence**

The danger that evidence will lure the factfinder into deciding on an improper emotional or irrational basis. A judge may exclude even relevant evidence when unfair prejudice substantially outweighs probative value.

*In use:* The graphic photos had some relevance, but the judge excluded them for unfair prejudice.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/relevant_evidence) · related: probative value, relevant evidence

### `witness`

**Evidence**

A person who gives testimony based on personal knowledge, expertise, or another legally permitted basis. Witness rules cover competence, oath, examination, and credibility.

*In use:* The only witness to the signing was the paralegal.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/witness) · related: testimony, expert witness
