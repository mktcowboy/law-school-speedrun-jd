# Contracts Vocabulary

Back to the [doctrine vocabulary index](./README.md).

High-frequency contracts terms for reading cases, building outlines, and spotting exam issues.

### `acceptance`

**Contracts**

Acceptance is the offeree's assent to the terms of an offer in the manner the offer invites or requires. Once valid acceptance occurs, the parties usually have a contract.

*In use:* Mailing the signed purchase order counted as acceptance because the offer allowed acceptance by return mail.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/acceptance) · related: offer, mutual assent, consideration

### `accord and satisfaction`

**Contracts**

Accord and satisfaction is an agreement to settle an existing claim or duty by giving and accepting different performance. The accord is the new agreement, and satisfaction is performance of it.

*In use:* Cashing the check marked "payment in full" raised an accord-and-satisfaction issue.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/accord_and_satisfaction) · related: breach of contract, consideration, settlement

### `adhesion contract`

**Contracts**

An adhesion contract is a standardized take-it-or-leave-it agreement offered by a party with stronger bargaining power. Courts usually enforce these contracts, but unfair surprise or oppressive terms can trigger closer scrutiny.

*In use:* The phone-service terms were analyzed as an adhesion contract because customers had no real chance to negotiate.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/adhesion_contract) · related: unconscionability, duress, interpretation

### `anticipatory repudiation`

**Contracts**

Anticipatory repudiation occurs when a party clearly indicates before performance is due that the party will not perform. The other party may treat that statement or conduct as a breach and pursue remedies.

*In use:* The seller's email saying it would not deliver next month was anticipatory repudiation.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/breach_of_contract) · related: breach of contract, damages, mitigation

### `assignment`

**Contracts**

Assignment transfers a contractual right from one party to another. After a valid assignment, the assignee can usually enforce the assigned right against the obligor.

*In use:* The contractor assigned its right to payment to the bank that financed the project.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/assignment) · related: delegation, third-party beneficiary, contract rights

### `bilateral contract`

**Contracts**

A bilateral contract is formed by an exchange of promises. Most ordinary sales and service agreements are bilateral because each side promises future performance.

*In use:* The lease was bilateral because the landlord promised possession and the tenant promised rent.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/bilateral_contract) · related: unilateral contract, offer, acceptance

### `breach of contract`

**Contracts**

Breach of contract is failure to perform a duty required by a valid contract. A breach can be minor, material, total, or anticipatory depending on its timing and seriousness.

*In use:* Delivering the equipment three weeks late was pleaded as breach of contract.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/breach_of_contract) · related: material breach, damages, substantial performance

### `capacity`

**Contracts**

Capacity is legal ability to enter a binding contract. Minors, some intoxicated persons, and some people with mental incapacity may be able to avoid agreements.

*In use:* The court considered capacity because the buyer was seventeen when she signed the contract.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/capacity) · related: voidable contract, assent, infancy

### `condition precedent`

**Contracts**

A condition precedent is an event that must occur before a contractual duty becomes due. If the condition never happens, the duty may never mature.

*In use:* Financing approval was a condition precedent to the buyer's duty to close.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/condition_precedent) · related: condition subsequent, performance, breach

### `condition subsequent`

**Contracts**

A condition subsequent is an event that cuts off an existing contractual duty after it has arisen. It is less common than a condition precedent but shows up in termination clauses.

*In use:* The insurance policy treated failure to provide timely proof of loss as a condition subsequent.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/condition_subsequent) · related: condition precedent, performance, termination

### `consideration`

**Contracts**

Consideration is the bargained-for legal value exchanged to support a contract. It can be a promise, act, forbearance, or change in legal position.

*In use:* The promise to pay was supported by consideration because the painter promised to repaint the house.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/consideration) · related: mutual assent, promissory estoppel, contract

### `counteroffer`

**Contracts**

A counteroffer rejects the original offer and proposes different terms. Under common-law rules, it usually terminates the offeree's power to accept the original offer.

*In use:* Asking to buy the car for $8,000 instead of $9,000 was a counteroffer, not acceptance.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/counteroffer) · related: offer, acceptance, mirror image rule

### `delegation`

**Contracts**

Delegation transfers performance of a contractual duty to another person. The delegating party usually remains liable unless the obligee agrees to a novation.

*In use:* The general contractor delegated plumbing work to a subcontractor but remained responsible to the owner.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/delegation) · related: assignment, novation, performance

### `duress`

**Contracts**

Duress is improper pressure that leaves a party without a reasonable alternative but to agree. A contract formed by duress may be voidable.

*In use:* Threatening to breach at the last minute unless the buyer paid double raised economic-duress concerns.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/duress) · related: undue influence, unconscionability, voidable contract

### `express contract`

**Contracts**

An express contract is formed through words, either written or spoken, that state the parties' agreement. It differs from an implied contract, which is inferred from conduct.

*In use:* The signed employment agreement was an express contract.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/express_contract) · related: implied contract, mutual assent, interpretation

### `frustration of purpose`

**Contracts**

Frustration of purpose excuses performance when an unforeseen event destroys the main reason for the contract, even though performance is still possible. Courts apply it narrowly.

*In use:* The tenant argued frustration of purpose after the parade that motivated the balcony rental was canceled.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/frustration_of_purpose) · related: impossibility, impracticability, excuse

### `implied contract`

**Contracts**

An implied contract is inferred from the parties' conduct rather than stated words. The law treats the conduct as showing mutual assent to exchange.

*In use:* Ordering food and eating it created an implied contract to pay the posted price.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/implied_contract) · related: express contract, quasi-contract, mutual assent

### `impossibility`

**Contracts**

Impossibility excuses performance when an unexpected event makes the promised performance objectively impossible. It is not enough that performance became more expensive or inconvenient.

*In use:* Destruction of the unique concert hall before the show raised an impossibility defense.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/impossibility) · related: frustration of purpose, impracticability, excuse

### `liquidated damages`

**Contracts**

Liquidated damages are an agreed sum payable if a breach occurs. Courts generally enforce them when actual damages were hard to estimate and the amount is reasonable, not punitive.

*In use:* The construction contract set liquidated damages of $500 per day for late completion.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/liquidated_damages) · related: damages, penalty, breach of contract

### `material breach`

**Contracts**

A material breach is a serious failure of performance that defeats the contract's essential purpose. It can excuse the nonbreaching party's remaining duties and support broader remedies.

*In use:* Installing the wrong roof system was a material breach because it went to the heart of the bargain.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/breach_of_contract) · related: substantial performance, damages, condition

### `meeting of the minds`

**Contracts**

Meeting of the minds is shorthand for mutual assent. Modern doctrine usually asks whether outward words and conduct would make a reasonable person think the parties agreed.

*In use:* Secretly attaching different meanings to the same price term prevented a true meeting of the minds.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/mutual_assent) · related: mutual assent, offer, acceptance

### `mitigation`

**Contracts**

Mitigation is the injured party's duty to take reasonable steps to avoid avoidable losses after breach. Losses that could have been reasonably avoided may not be recoverable.

*In use:* The landlord had to try to relet the apartment to mitigate damages after the tenant moved out early.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/mitigation) · related: damages, breach of contract, cover

### `mutual assent`

**Contracts**

Mutual assent is agreement by both parties to the same bargain. Courts judge assent mostly from outward manifestations, not private thoughts.

*In use:* The emails showed mutual assent even though the parties planned to sign a fuller document later.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/mutual_assent) · related: offer, acceptance, consideration

### `offer`

**Contracts**

An offer is a manifestation of willingness to enter a bargain that gives another person power to accept. Advertisements and preliminary negotiations often are not offers.

*In use:* The written quote became an offer because it identified the goods, price, quantity, and invited acceptance.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/offer) · related: acceptance, counteroffer, option contract

### `option contract`

**Contracts**

An option contract keeps an offer open for a stated time in exchange for consideration or another recognized basis. The offeror cannot revoke during the option period.

*In use:* Paying $100 for thirty days to decide whether to buy the land created an option contract.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/option_contract) · related: offer, consideration, acceptance

### `parol evidence rule`

**Contracts**

The parol evidence rule limits use of earlier or contemporaneous outside statements to change a final written agreement. It does not bar evidence offered for every purpose, such as fraud, mistake, or interpreting ambiguity.

*In use:* The buyer could not use prior negotiations to contradict the integrated written price term.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/parol_evidence_rule) · related: integration, interpretation, ambiguity

### `promissory estoppel`

**Contracts**

Promissory estoppel can enforce a promise without ordinary consideration when reliance was reasonably expected and injustice can be avoided only by enforcement. It is often tested as a fallback theory.

*In use:* The subcontractor's bid supported promissory estoppel after the general contractor relied on it to win the project.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/promissory_estoppel) · related: consideration, reliance, restitution

### `quasi-contract`

**Contracts**

Quasi-contract is a restitutionary remedy imposed to prevent unjust enrichment, even when no true contract was formed. It asks what benefit was unfairly retained, not what bargain was made.

*In use:* The hospital sought quasi-contract recovery for emergency services provided to an unconscious patient.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/quasi_contract) · related: restitution, implied contract, unjust enrichment

### `rescission`

**Contracts**

Rescission unwinds a contract and tries to put the parties back where they were before the agreement. It is commonly tied to fraud, mistake, duress, or material breach.

*In use:* After discovering the misrepresentation, the buyer sought rescission instead of damages.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/rescission) · related: restitution, voidable contract, fraud

### `restitution`

**Contracts**

Restitution requires a party to give back a benefit unjustly retained. It can operate as a contract remedy or as an independent unjust-enrichment theory.

*In use:* The contractor sought restitution for the value of work accepted before the contract was avoided.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/restitution) · related: quasi-contract, rescission, damages

### `statute of frauds`

**Contracts**

The statute of frauds requires certain contracts to be evidenced by a signed writing. Common categories include land contracts, suretyship promises, and contracts that cannot be performed within one year.

*In use:* The oral land sale failed under the statute of frauds because no signed writing identified the property and price.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/statute_of_frauds) · related: writing, land sale contract, UCC

### `substantial performance`

**Contracts**

Substantial performance occurs when a party has performed enough that the other side must pay, subject to damages for defects. It usually applies when defects are not willful and do not defeat the contract's main purpose.

*In use:* The builder substantially performed despite minor trim defects that could be repaired.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/substantial_performance) · related: material breach, damages, performance

### `unconscionability`

**Contracts**

Unconscionability allows a court to refuse or limit enforcement of extremely unfair contract terms. Courts often look for both procedural unfairness in bargaining and substantive unfairness in the terms.

*In use:* The arbitration clause was challenged as unconscionable because it imposed one-sided costs on consumers.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/unconscionability) · related: adhesion contract, duress, public policy

### `undue influence`

**Contracts**

Undue influence is unfair persuasion by someone who dominates or holds special trust over another person. A contract produced by undue influence may be voidable.

*In use:* The caregiver's pressure on the isolated client raised an undue-influence issue.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/undue_influence) · related: duress, capacity, voidable contract

### `unilateral contract`

**Contracts**

A unilateral contract is formed when an offer invites acceptance by performance rather than by a return promise. The classic example is a reward offer.

*In use:* The lost-dog reward became a unilateral contract when the neighbor returned the dog.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/unilateral_contract) · related: bilateral contract, offer, acceptance

### `void contract`

**Contracts**

A void contract has no legal effect from the start. Courts treat it as though no enforceable contract ever existed.

*In use:* An agreement to commit a crime is a void contract.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/void_contract) · related: voidable contract, illegality, public policy

### `voidable contract`

**Contracts**

A voidable contract is valid unless the protected party chooses to avoid it. Capacity problems, duress, fraud, and undue influence often make contracts voidable rather than automatically void.

*In use:* The minor's laptop purchase was voidable at the minor's election.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/voidable_contract) · related: capacity, duress, rescission

### `warranty`

**Contracts**

A warranty is a contractual assurance about facts, quality, title, or performance. In sales, warranties often determine what the buyer can recover when goods do not conform.

*In use:* The buyer claimed the machine breached the warranty that it could process 500 units per hour.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/warranty) · related: breach of contract, UCC, products liability
