# Torts Vocabulary

Back to the [doctrine vocabulary index](./README.md).

High-frequency torts terms for reading cases, building outlines, and spotting exam issues.

### `actual cause`

**Torts**

Actual cause asks whether the injury would have happened without the defendant's conduct. It is the factual link before the court asks whether the connection is legally close enough.

*In use:* The plaintiff could show actual cause because the crash would not have happened but for the defendant running the red light.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/actual_cause) · related: causation, proximate cause, superseding cause

### `assault`

**Torts**

Assault is an intentional act that makes another person reasonably expect imminent harmful or offensive contact. The threatened contact matters even if no touching occurs.

*In use:* Raising a fist and stepping toward the plaintiff could support assault if the plaintiff reasonably expected an immediate hit.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/assault) · related: battery, intent, consent

### `assumption of risk`

**Torts**

Assumption of risk limits or bars recovery when the plaintiff knowingly and voluntarily accepted a particular danger. It can appear as an express waiver or as conduct showing the plaintiff chose to face the risk.

*In use:* A skier who ignored posted warnings about a closed trail may face an assumption-of-risk defense.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/assumption_of_risk) · related: comparative negligence, contributory negligence, consent

### `battery`

**Torts**

Battery is an intentional harmful or offensive contact with another person. The contact can be direct or indirect, such as causing an object to strike the plaintiff.

*In use:* Snatching a plate from someone's hands can be battery if it is offensive contact with something closely connected to the person.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/battery) · related: assault, intent, consent

### `breach`

**Torts**

Breach is the defendant's failure to meet the required standard of care. In negligence, it means the defendant acted less carefully than a reasonable person would under the circumstances.

*In use:* The store breached its duty by leaving a spill unmarked after employees had time to discover it.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/breach) · related: duty of care, negligence, reasonable person

### `causation`

**Torts**

Causation connects the defendant's wrong to the plaintiff's injury. Torts usually separates it into factual cause and proximate cause.

*In use:* Even with a clear duty and breach, the plaintiff still had to prove causation.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/causation) · related: actual cause, proximate cause, intervening cause

### `comparative negligence`

**Torts**

Comparative negligence reduces the plaintiff's recovery by the plaintiff's share of fault. Some jurisdictions still bar recovery if the plaintiff's fault crosses a set threshold.

*In use:* The jury found the plaintiff 30 percent at fault, so comparative negligence reduced the damages award.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/comparative_negligence) · related: contributory negligence, assumption of risk, damages

### `consent`

**Torts**

Consent is permission that can defeat an intentional tort claim when the defendant stays within the scope of what was allowed. Consent may be express, implied from conduct, or limited by circumstances.

*In use:* The athlete consented to ordinary contact during the game, but not to an intentional punch after the whistle.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/consent) · related: battery, assumption of risk, privilege

### `contributory negligence`

**Torts**

Contributory negligence is the older rule that can bar a plaintiff from recovery if the plaintiff's own negligence helped cause the injury. Only a small number of jurisdictions still use it in its strict form.

*In use:* In a contributory-negligence jurisdiction, even slight plaintiff fault can be a complete defense.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/contributory_negligence) · related: comparative negligence, assumption of risk, negligence

### `conversion`

**Torts**

Conversion is serious interference with another person's personal property, treated as if the defendant has taken ownership of it. The usual remedy is the value of the property, not just repair.

*In use:* Selling a borrowed laptop without permission is a classic conversion problem.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/conversion) · related: trespass to chattels, damages, property

### `defamation`

**Torts**

Defamation is a false statement of fact that harms another person's reputation. Written defamation is often called libel, while spoken defamation is often called slander.

*In use:* The plaintiff pleaded defamation after the article falsely accused her of professional misconduct.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/defamation) · related: libel, slander, damages

### `design defect`

**Torts**

A design defect exists when a product is unsafe because of the way the whole product line was planned, not because one item was made incorrectly. Courts often ask whether a safer reasonable alternative design was available or whether consumers would expect the danger.

*In use:* The plaintiff argued that the guardrail had a design defect because every unit shared the same dangerous end cap.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/products_liability) · related: manufacturing defect, products liability, strict liability

### `duty of care`

**Torts**

Duty of care is the legal obligation to act with the caution the law requires toward another person. Without duty, negligence analysis usually stops before breach and causation.

*In use:* The court first asked whether the landlord owed a duty of care to guests in the common hallway.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/duty_of_care) · related: breach, negligence, reasonable person

### `false imprisonment`

**Torts**

False imprisonment is intentionally confining someone within boundaries without lawful privilege and without consent. Physical barriers, threats, or asserted legal authority can all be enough.

*In use:* Locking the customer in a back office during questioning raised a false-imprisonment claim.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/false_imprisonment) · related: intent, consent, privilege

### `foreseeability`

**Torts**

Foreseeability asks whether a reasonable person could anticipate the type of harm that occurred. It matters in duty, breach, proximate cause, and some defenses.

*In use:* The court treated the slipping hazard as foreseeable because customers routinely tracked rainwater into the lobby.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/foreseeability) · related: proximate cause, duty of care, reasonable person

### `intentional infliction of emotional distress`

**Torts**

Intentional infliction of emotional distress is extreme and outrageous conduct that intentionally or recklessly causes severe emotional harm. Ordinary insults usually are not enough.

*In use:* Repeated threats exploiting the plaintiff's known vulnerability supported an intentional-infliction claim.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/intentional_infliction_of_emotional_distress) · related: intent, damages, tort

### `intervening cause`

**Torts**

An intervening cause is an event that occurs after the defendant's conduct and contributes to the injury. It matters because some later events are foreseeable, while others may cut off liability.

*In use:* The defendant argued that the later criminal act was an intervening cause of the plaintiff's injury.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/intervening_cause) · related: proximate cause, superseding cause, causation

### `invitee`

**Torts**

An invitee is someone invited onto land for the possessor's business purpose or for a purpose open to the public. Traditionally, land possessors owe invitees the highest premises-liability duty.

*In use:* A grocery shopper is usually treated as an invitee while shopping during store hours.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/invitee) · related: licensee, trespasser, premises liability

### `licensee`

**Torts**

A licensee is someone allowed to enter land for the licensee's own purpose, such as a social guest. Traditional doctrine requires warning licensees about hidden dangers the possessor knows about.

*In use:* A dinner guest injured by a concealed broken step may be analyzed as a licensee.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/licensee) · related: invitee, trespasser, premises liability

### `manufacturing defect`

**Torts**

A manufacturing defect exists when a product departs from its intended design and becomes unsafe. The problem is with a particular item or batch, not the design as a whole.

*In use:* The tire blowout case turned on whether one tire had a manufacturing defect.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/products_liability) · related: design defect, products liability, strict liability

### `negligence`

**Torts**

Negligence is failing to use legally required care and thereby causing harm. The basic elements are duty, breach, causation, and damages.

*In use:* The plaintiff's negligence claim failed because she could not show the broken railing caused the fall.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/negligence) · related: duty of care, breach, causation

### `negligence per se`

**Torts**

Negligence per se lets a statute set the standard of care when the statute protects the class of people and type of harm involved. If it applies, violating the statute can establish breach.

*In use:* Driving without headlights after dark supported negligence per se when the collision involved the visibility risk the statute addressed.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/negligence_per_se) · related: negligence, breach, duty of care

### `nuisance`

**Torts**

Nuisance is interference with the use and enjoyment of land or with rights shared by the public. The doctrine splits into private nuisance and public nuisance.

*In use:* Constant smoke crossing onto the neighbor's land was pleaded as nuisance.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/nuisance) · related: private nuisance, public nuisance, property

### `premises liability`

**Torts**

Premises liability covers injuries caused by conditions on land or buildings. Traditional rules vary the land possessor's duty based on whether the plaintiff is an invitee, licensee, or trespasser.

*In use:* The icy entrance made the case a premises-liability problem rather than an ordinary car-accident negligence claim.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/negligence) · related: invitee, licensee, trespasser

### `private nuisance`

**Torts**

Private nuisance is a substantial and unreasonable interference with another person's use or enjoyment of land. It often focuses on recurring conditions like smoke, noise, vibration, or odors.

*In use:* The late-night factory noise supported a private-nuisance claim by nearby homeowners.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/private_nuisance) · related: nuisance, public nuisance, property

### `products liability`

**Torts**

Products liability is the field governing injuries caused by defective products. Claims may involve manufacturing defects, design defects, failure to warn, negligence, warranty, or strict liability.

*In use:* The exploding battery case raised products-liability theories against the manufacturer and seller.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/products_liability) · related: strict liability, design defect, manufacturing defect

### `proximate cause`

**Torts**

Proximate cause asks whether the defendant's conduct is legally close enough to the injury for liability. Courts often frame the question through foreseeability and intervening events.

*In use:* The court held that the unusual chain of events broke proximate cause.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/proximate_cause) · related: actual cause, foreseeability, superseding cause

### `public nuisance`

**Torts**

Public nuisance is an unreasonable interference with a right held by the community, such as public health, safety, or access. A private plaintiff usually needs special harm different from the general public's harm.

*In use:* Blocking a public road with debris could be treated as a public nuisance.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/public_nuisance) · related: nuisance, private nuisance, injunction

### `reasonable person`

**Torts**

The reasonable person is the objective benchmark for ordinary care. The question is not what this defendant thought was careful, but what a reasonably prudent person would have done.

*In use:* The jury compared the driver's conduct to the reasonable person under the same road conditions.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/reasonable_person) · related: negligence, breach, duty of care

### `res ipsa loquitur`

**Torts** · lit. "the thing speaks for itself"

Res ipsa loquitur allows negligence to be inferred from the nature of an accident when the event ordinarily would not occur without negligence and the defendant likely controlled the instrumentality. It helps plaintiffs when direct evidence of the precise breach is unavailable.

*In use:* A surgical sponge left inside a patient is the kind of fact pattern that invites res ipsa loquitur.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/res_ipsa_loquitur) · related: negligence, breach, evidence

### `respondeat superior`

**Torts** · lit. "let the superior answer"

Respondeat superior makes an employer vicariously liable for torts committed by an employee within the scope of employment. The doctrine does not usually cover purely personal detours.

*In use:* The delivery company faced respondeat superior liability for the driver's crash during a scheduled route.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/respondeat_superior) · related: vicarious liability, scope of employment, agency

### `strict liability`

**Torts**

Strict liability imposes responsibility without requiring proof that the defendant acted carelessly or intended harm. It commonly appears in abnormally dangerous activities, certain animal cases, and products liability.

*In use:* The blasting company could face strict liability even if it used reasonable care.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/strict_liability) · related: products liability, negligence, damages

### `superseding cause`

**Torts**

A superseding cause is an intervening event so unexpected or independent that it cuts off the defendant's liability. It is a proximate-cause concept, not a factual-cause concept.

*In use:* The defendant claimed the arson after the collision was a superseding cause of the building loss.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/superseding_cause) · related: intervening cause, proximate cause, causation

### `tort`

**Torts**

A tort is a civil wrong, other than breach of contract, for which the law provides a remedy. Torts can be intentional, negligent, or based on strict liability.

*In use:* Battery is an intentional tort, while ordinary car-crash liability is usually negligence.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/tort) · related: negligence, damages, civil liability

### `trespass`

**Torts**

Trespass is an intentional physical invasion of another person's land. The entry can be by a person, object, or sometimes a substance.

*In use:* Throwing debris onto the neighbor's yard can count as trespass even without stepping across the boundary.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/trespass) · related: property, nuisance, damages

### `trespass to chattels`

**Torts**

Trespass to chattels is intentional interference with another person's personal property that causes dispossession, damage, or loss of use. It is less serious than conversion.

*In use:* Temporarily disabling another person's phone may be trespass to chattels if it causes measurable loss of use.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/trespass_to_chattels) · related: conversion, personal property, damages

### `vicarious liability`

**Torts**

Vicarious liability makes one person legally responsible for another person's tort because of their relationship. The most common version is employer liability for employees acting within the scope of employment.

*In use:* The plaintiff sued the hospital on a vicarious-liability theory for the nurse's negligence.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/vicarious_liability) · related: respondeat superior, agency, scope of employment
