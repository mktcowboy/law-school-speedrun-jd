# Constitutional Law Vocabulary

Back to the [doctrine vocabulary index](./README.md) · [vocabulary overview](../README.md).

Core terms for the 1L constitutional law course: judicial review, justiciability, federal power, individual rights, scrutiny, and structural limits.

### `bill of attainder`

**Constitutional Law**

A legislative act that punishes a specific person or group without a judicial trial. The Constitution bars both federal and state bills of attainder.

*In use:* The statute looked like a bill of attainder because it singled out one named company for punishment.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/bill_of_attainder) · related: ex post facto, separation of powers

### `case or controversy`

**Constitutional Law**

The Article III limit that federal courts decide concrete disputes, not abstract legal questions. Standing, ripeness, mootness, and political question doctrine all grow from this idea.

*In use:* The advisory request failed because there was no case or controversy.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/justiciability) · related: standing, mootness

### `Commerce Clause`

**Constitutional Law**

Congress's power to regulate commerce with foreign nations, among the states, and with Native tribes. Much of federal regulatory power is tested against this clause.

*In use:* Congress relied on the Commerce Clause to regulate the national market.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/commerce_clause) · related: Necessary and Proper Clause, dormant Commerce Clause

### `content-based restriction`

**Constitutional Law**

A speech regulation that applies because of what the speaker says or the subject discussed. Content-based laws usually trigger strict scrutiny.

*In use:* A permit rule covering only political signs was a content-based restriction.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/freedom_of_speech) · related: strict scrutiny, viewpoint discrimination

### `dormant Commerce Clause`

**Constitutional Law**

The principle that state laws may not discriminate against or unduly burden interstate commerce, even when Congress has not legislated. It is inferred from the Commerce Clause structure.

*In use:* The local-only licensing rule raised a dormant Commerce Clause problem.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/dormant_commerce_clause) · related: Commerce Clause, preemption

### `Due Process Clause`

**Constitutional Law**

The Fifth and Fourteenth Amendment protection against deprivation of life, liberty, or property without due process of law. It has both procedural and substantive branches.

*In use:* Terminating benefits without a hearing violated the Due Process Clause.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/due_process) · related: procedural due process, substantive due process

### `Equal Protection Clause`

**Constitutional Law**

The Fourteenth Amendment command that states treat similarly situated people alike unless a sufficient justification supports different treatment. The level of scrutiny depends on the classification and right involved.

*In use:* The residency rule was challenged under the Equal Protection Clause.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/equal_protection) · related: strict scrutiny, rational basis review

### `Establishment Clause`

**Constitutional Law**

The First Amendment limit on government establishment of religion. It restricts official endorsement, coercion, and certain forms of government support for religion.

*In use:* The school prayer policy triggered an Establishment Clause challenge.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/establishment_clause) · related: Free Exercise Clause, First Amendment

### `ex post facto`

**Constitutional Law**

A retroactive criminal law that makes past conduct criminal, increases punishment, or changes rules to the defendant's disadvantage. The Constitution forbids ex post facto laws.

*In use:* Increasing the penalty after the crime raised an ex post facto issue.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/ex_post_facto) · related: bill of attainder, criminal law

### `Free Exercise Clause`

**Constitutional Law**

The First Amendment protection for religious belief and practice. Neutral, generally applicable laws are usually valid even if they burden religion, but targeted burdens face strict scrutiny.

*In use:* The ordinance singled out religious practice and violated the Free Exercise Clause.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/free_exercise_clause) · related: Establishment Clause, strict scrutiny

### `free speech`

**Constitutional Law**

The First Amendment protection against government suppression of expression. Doctrine varies by forum, medium, speaker, and whether the regulation targets content.

*In use:* The protest ban was analyzed as a free speech restriction.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/freedom_of_speech) · related: prior restraint, overbreadth

### `fundamental right`

**Constitutional Law**

A right treated as especially important under the Constitution, often triggering strict scrutiny when substantially burdened. Examples include voting, interstate travel, and certain privacy or family rights.

*In use:* Because marriage is a fundamental right, the state needed more than a weak justification.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/fundamental_right) · related: substantive due process, strict scrutiny

### `incorporation doctrine`

**Constitutional Law**

The doctrine applying most Bill of Rights protections against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. Modern incorporation is mostly selective, right by right.

*In use:* The incorporation doctrine made the First Amendment binding on the state.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/incorporation_doctrine) · related: Fourteenth Amendment, due process

### `intermediate scrutiny`

**Constitutional Law**

A middle level of review requiring an important government interest and means substantially related to that interest. It often appears in sex-classification and some speech cases.

*In use:* The gender classification had to survive intermediate scrutiny.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/intermediate_scrutiny) · related: strict scrutiny, rational basis review

### `judicial review`

**Constitutional Law**

The power of courts to declare government action unconstitutional. Marbury v. Madison is the canonical starting point.

*In use:* Judicial review allowed the Court to invalidate the statute.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/judicial_review) · related: supremacy, separation of powers

### `justiciability`

**Constitutional Law**

The set of doctrines deciding whether a federal court may hear a dispute. Standing, ripeness, mootness, and political question doctrine are the big recurring pieces.

*In use:* The court dismissed on justiciability grounds before reaching the merits.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/justiciability) · related: standing, case or controversy

### `mootness`

**Constitutional Law**

A justiciability doctrine requiring a live controversy throughout the case. If events make the court unable to grant meaningful relief, the case is usually moot.

*In use:* Repeal of the challenged rule made the injunction claim moot.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/mootness) · related: ripeness, standing

### `Necessary and Proper Clause`

**Constitutional Law**

Congress's power to use appropriate means to carry out its enumerated powers. It expands how Congress may execute granted powers but does not create a free-standing police power.

*In use:* The bank was upheld under the Necessary and Proper Clause as a means to fiscal powers.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/necessary_and_proper_clause) · related: Commerce Clause, enumerated powers

### `overbreadth`

**Constitutional Law**

A First Amendment doctrine allowing a law to be invalidated because it sweeps in too much protected speech. It is powerful because a litigant may raise the rights of speakers not before the court.

*In use:* The ban on all online criticism of officials was struck for overbreadth.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/overbreadth) · related: vagueness, free speech

### `police powers`

**Constitutional Law**

The states' general authority to regulate for health, safety, welfare, and morals. Congress has enumerated powers instead of a general federal police power.

*In use:* The quarantine order rested on the state's police powers.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/police_powers) · related: federalism, Commerce Clause

### `political question doctrine`

**Constitutional Law**

A justiciability doctrine keeping federal courts out of issues constitutionally committed to the political branches or lacking manageable legal standards. It is about institutional role, not political importance.

*In use:* The impeachment challenge was dismissed under the political question doctrine.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/political_question_doctrine) · related: justiciability, separation of powers

### `preemption`

**Constitutional Law**

The rule that valid federal law displaces conflicting state law under the Supremacy Clause. Preemption may be express, field, or conflict-based.

*In use:* The federal labeling statute preempted the state warning requirement.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/preemption) · related: supremacy, federalism

### `prior restraint`

**Constitutional Law**

Government restriction of speech before it occurs, such as licensing or an injunction against publication. Prior restraints are strongly disfavored under the First Amendment.

*In use:* The order blocking publication was treated as a prior restraint.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/prior_restraint) · related: free speech, content-based restriction

### `procedural due process`

**Constitutional Law**

The required procedures before government deprives someone of life, liberty, or property. The basic idea is notice and an opportunity to be heard at a meaningful time and in a meaningful way.

*In use:* A post-termination hearing was too late for procedural due process.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/procedural_due_process) · related: due process, hearing

### `rational basis review`

**Constitutional Law**

The most deferential level of constitutional review. A law survives if it is rationally related to a legitimate government interest.

*In use:* Economic regulation usually receives rational basis review.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/rational_basis_test) · related: intermediate scrutiny, strict scrutiny

### `ripeness`

**Constitutional Law**

A justiciability doctrine asking whether a dispute is developed enough for judicial review. Courts avoid premature decisions based on contingent future events.

*In use:* The pre-enforcement challenge was ripe because penalties were imminent.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/ripeness) · related: mootness, standing

### `separation of powers`

**Constitutional Law**

The allocation of federal authority among Congress, the President, and the courts. The doctrine polices both encroachment by one branch and improper delegation of power.

*In use:* The removal restriction raised a separation of powers problem.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/separation_of_powers) · related: judicial review, political question doctrine

### `standing`

**Constitutional Law**

The requirement that a plaintiff have injury in fact, causation, and redressability. It ensures the plaintiff has a concrete stake in the dispute.

*In use:* The voter had standing because the districting plan directly affected her vote.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/standing) · related: justiciability, case or controversy

### `state action`

**Constitutional Law**

The requirement that constitutional rights generally constrain government, not purely private conduct. Private conduct can count when it is sufficiently connected to the state.

*In use:* The private mall's conduct lacked state action.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/state_action) · related: Fourteenth Amendment, civil rights

### `strict scrutiny`

**Constitutional Law**

The most demanding level of constitutional review. The government must show a compelling interest and narrow tailoring, often using the least restrictive means.

*In use:* The racial classification triggered strict scrutiny.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/strict_scrutiny) · related: suspect classification, fundamental right

### `substantive due process`

**Constitutional Law**

The doctrine protecting certain fundamental liberties from government interference regardless of the procedure used. It often overlaps with privacy, family, bodily autonomy, and economic-liberty debates.

*In use:* The parents framed the right to direct education as substantive due process.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/substantive_due_process) · related: fundamental right, due process

### `suspect classification`

**Constitutional Law**

A classification, such as race or national origin, that courts treat as especially likely to reflect unconstitutional prejudice. Suspect classifications usually trigger strict scrutiny.

*In use:* The race-based admissions rule involved a suspect classification.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/strict_scrutiny) · related: equal protection, strict scrutiny

### `Takings Clause`

**Constitutional Law**

The Fifth Amendment rule that private property may not be taken for public use without just compensation. It applies to physical takings, regulatory takings, and some exactions.

*In use:* The permanent access requirement was analyzed under the Takings Clause.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/takings_clause) · related: just compensation, property

### `vagueness`

**Constitutional Law**

A due process doctrine invalidating laws so unclear that ordinary people cannot tell what is prohibited or enforcement is left too discretionary. It appears often in criminal and speech cases.

*In use:* The loitering ordinance failed for vagueness.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/vagueness_doctrine) · related: overbreadth, due process

### `viewpoint discrimination`

**Constitutional Law**

A speech restriction that favors or disfavors a particular perspective within a subject area. It is especially suspect under the First Amendment.

*In use:* Allowing pro-war signs but banning anti-war signs was viewpoint discrimination.

See: [Wex](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/freedom_of_speech) · related: content-based restriction, free speech
